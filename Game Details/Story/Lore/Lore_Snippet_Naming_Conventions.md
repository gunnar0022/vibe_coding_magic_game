# LORE SNIPPET — What They Call It: Naming Conventions and Magical Cultures

## The Principle

Every community that has discovered magic in the past year has given it a name. The name they chose reveals everything — how they found it, what they think it is, how they relate to it, and where their understanding breaks down. No two communities arrived at the same conclusion independently because no two communities had the same first experience. The word is a worldview compressed into a syllable.

When the player arrives at a new settlement and hears what they call magic, that single word is a diagnostic. It tells the player how these people think, what they've seen, what they haven't seen, and what they'll fight to defend as truth.

---

## The Names

### Magic

The most common term. The old-world word for the impossible, dusted off and applied to the newly possible. Communities that use this word tend to be the most pragmatic — they're not making grand claims about the nature of what they've found. They're labeling what they see. It works. They don't know why. They call it what their grandparents' stories would have called it.

This word carries the least ideological weight, which makes "magic" communities the easiest for outsiders to interact with. There's no theological framework to navigate, no cosmology to respect. Just people doing things they couldn't do a year ago and calling it the obvious thing. The downside is that pragmatism can mask shallow understanding. A community that shrugs and says "it's magic" may not be asking the questions that would keep them safe.

### Divinity / Prayer / God

A community that discovered magic through resonance — someone in a moment of deep need or overwhelming emotion felt the ambient field respond without understanding what happened — would naturally interpret that as answered prayer. They didn't study a symbol. They didn't find a ruin. They *asked* and something *answered*. From their perspective, that isn't a system. It's a relationship with a conscious being.

The kanji they later discover aren't symbols — they're prayers. The specific words She responds to. Casting isn't channeling. It's worship. The mana pool isn't an ecological resource. It's Her presence, stronger in some places because She favors certain ground, weaker in others because She has withdrawn.

This framework produces a completely unique relationship to every aspect of the magic system:

**Hollowing** isn't reckless overcasting. It's being forsaken. She withdrew Her presence and you kept praying and the cost came from your own soul. Within this theology, that could be interpreted as divine punishment, divine testing, or proof of insufficient faith. Different members of the same community might hold different interpretations, producing internal theological debate about the nature of suffering that mirrors real-world religious discourse — all built on a misunderstanding of a mechanical system.

**Corrupted beings** aren't people who hollowed themselves out. They're the damned. People who abused Her gift, or who prayed for the wrong things, or who were never truly faithful. The theological implications of the corruption spectrum — simple desperation producing bestial forms, complex motives producing intelligent horrors — would be interpreted through moral judgment. Simple corrupted creatures are simple sinners. Complex ones committed complex sins. The framework fits, horrifyingly well, even though the underlying model is wrong.

**The researcher and mentor** would be viewed with suspicion or hostility by this community. Studying Her gift analytically is somewhere between irreverent and blasphemous. You don't dissect a prayer. You don't test God. The tension between the Divinity framework and the Knowledge path is inherent and potentially volatile.

This community could be explored deeply. The theology is internally consistent, emotionally compelling, and almost entirely wrong about mechanism while being occasionally right about outcome. The player should find it hard to dismiss entirely, because the people practicing it are sincere, their community often functions well, and some of their instincts about respect and restraint genuinely align with how the system works.

### Elemancy

A community with a narrow discovery experience. They found kanji that produce elemental effects — fire, water, earth, wind — and concluded that element manipulation is what magic *is*. Their framework is confident, internally consistent, and fundamentally incomplete.

Elemancers might genuinely not believe non-elemental magic exists. Show them a healing spell and they'll try to fit it into their model — "that's water-element purification" or "that's earth-element body reinforcement." Show them something that truly can't be categorized elementally and they'll either reject it as fake or experience a crisis of understanding. Their framework is a box they've built around their own experience, and they mistake the box for the world.

The strength of this community is focus. Because they believe only elemental magic is real, they've poured all their experimentation into elemental kanji. They may have discovered pairings and techniques within the elemental domain that broader-minded communities missed simply because nobody else was looking that hard in that specific direction. Narrow belief, deep expertise.

The weakness is rigidity. When reality exceeds their model — and it will — they either adapt or break. An Elemancy community encountering the hollowing for the first time has no framework for it. Elements don't corrupt people. Fire doesn't hollow your soul. Their entire explanatory structure fails at the moment they need it most.

### The Flow

A community that discovered magic through environmental observation rather than personal casting. They noticed the movement of it first — the way it circulates, pools, thins out in some areas and concentrates in others. To them, magic isn't a tool or a deity. It's a natural phenomenon. A current. You don't *use* the Flow any more than you use a river. You position yourself within it and work with its movement.

This framework produces the healthiest instinctive relationship with mana ecology of any community. Their entire understanding is built around respecting a current rather than drawing from a pool. Aggressive casting — pulling hard, draining an area, forcing magic through a funnel regardless of local availability — feels as wrong to them as damming a river feels to a fisherman. They might be genuinely appalled watching a power-path caster drain a zone, not because they understand the hollowing risk, but because it violates their foundational sense of how magic should be interacted with.

The Flow community's weakness is passivity. Their respect for the current can become deference to it. If the Flow is weak here, they move. If the Flow doesn't support a particular casting, they don't force it. This makes them safe but potentially helpless in a crisis that demands pushing past the environment's natural capacity. They might lack the will to cast aggressively even when aggression is survival.

### Breath

A community that noticed the regenerative cycle. Mana depletes and returns. The land empties and fills. To them, magic is literally the world breathing — an inhale and an exhale, a rhythm as fundamental as a heartbeat.

Casting, in their understanding, is participating in that breath. You cast on the exhale — when the world is giving, when the mana is flowing outward and available. You rest on the inhale — when the world is drawing back in, recovering, refilling. If you try to cast when the land is breathing in, you're working against the rhythm, and the cost comes from somewhere it shouldn't.

This maps onto the mana regeneration cycle almost perfectly. The Breath community has independently invented a framework that respects exactly the right principle — the finite pool, the regeneration period, the danger of casting during depletion — without understanding the mechanism underneath. Their timing instincts are among the most accurate of any community. They know *when* to cast and when to stop, even if their explanation for why is poetic rather than mechanical.

Their potential weakness is rhythmic thinking applied to situations that don't follow a rhythm. Not every mana fluctuation is a breath. A zone drained by a corrupted being doesn't "inhale" on schedule. The Breath framework assumes regularity in a system that doesn't always provide it. When the rhythm breaks, so does their confidence.

### Resonance

A community that discovered magic through collective experience. At some point in the past year, a large group of people came together — singing, mourning, celebrating, something that unified them emotionally — and the ambient magic responded. Not to any individual. To the group. To the combined emotional density of many souls acting as one.

This is genuinely touching something real and distinct from standard kanji casting. When a large group of people synchronize emotionally — truly feeling the same thing, moving together, becoming one in the moment — their souls do something that a single person's soul cannot. On some level of interaction with the ambient field, the souls connect. They form a collective surface that catches ambient magic the way a sail catches wind, where a single person is just a thread in the breeze. The result is magic that doesn't flow through a kanji funnel. It flows through the *group*, and the effects are more vague, more abstract, more unpredictable than anything a targeted spell produces.

This can't be faked or trivially reproduced. The participants have to genuinely feel it. Mechanical repetition of a song without emotional unity produces nothing. A group going through the motions is just a group making noise. The resonance requires authentic collective experience — shared grief, shared joy, shared purpose felt deeply enough that individual boundaries soften and the souls briefly act as one.

The Resonance community is probably the most vocal in their casting practice. They sing, they chant, they use sound as the medium through which collective focus is built and maintained. Individual members cast normally on their own — visualizing kanji, channeling through the funnel like anyone else. Someone casting a fire spell might shout "Fire!" while holding the kanji in their mind, the vocalization serving as personal focus rather than mechanical necessity. But their distinctive practice, the thing that defines them, is the collective casting that no other community has discovered.

What this community is working with, whether they know it or not, is closer to the Skelemancer's formation principle than to standard casting. The Skelemancer formed when dense ambient emotional energy organized magic without a conscious participant. The Resonance community is doing something similar but controlled — dense collective emotional energy organizing magic through many conscious participants acting as one. The mechanism is related. The intent and outcome are vastly different. But the underlying principle — that emotional density can shape magic outside the kanji funnel system — is the same.

This community might understand something about magic that nobody else does, including the researcher and mentor. Their practice points toward a dimension of the system that individual casting can't access. Whether that understanding ever becomes formalized or remains embedded in the lived experience of communal singing is an open question.

The effects of collective resonance are broad but shallow. When the singing truly connects — when the group achieves genuine emotional unity — the result washes over the area like warm water. Small wounds close. Bruises fade. A persistent cough clears. Sore muscles ease. The feeling is restoration, not intervention — closer to a long rest in a hot spring than to targeted medicine. Nobody is cured of anything serious. Nobody's broken bone is mended. But everyone within the effect feels distinctly better in the way that accumulates into something meaningful over time. A community that resonates regularly is healthier, more resilient, and recovers faster from hardship — not through any single dramatic act of healing, but through a constant, gentle maintenance that no individual caster with any known kanji has been able to replicate. The complexity of true healing — cellular repair, mending what is broken at a structural level — remains beyond what anyone has achieved through the funnel system. The Resonance community hasn't solved that problem either. They've just found something adjacent: a collective comfort that touches everything lightly rather than fixing anything deeply.

### The Script

A community that found the kanji first — the physical symbols on old-world artifacts — and built their entire understanding around the written form. To them, magic is fundamentally textual. The symbols aren't an interface to something larger. They *are* the magic. The writing is the power. The ink on the page is what matters.

This community hasn't realized that you don't need to be visually engaging with the symbol to cast it. They believe the written form must be seen — looked at, read, visually held — for magic to work. The kanji on the page and the kanji in the mind must be present simultaneously. The eye and the soul working together.

In practice, this produces a distinctive casting style. Script casters carry scrolls — prepared kanji on portable surfaces, ready to be unrolled, stared at, and cast from. They whip out a scroll, lock their eyes on the symbol, and cast. The visual engagement reinforces the mental image, and the scroll becomes a rapid-access tool. A Script caster with a well-organized scroll kit can chain-cast spells quickly by flipping from one prepared symbol to the next, each visual contact refreshing and sharpening the mental image.

This is simultaneously a limitation and a genuine technique. The limitation: they're dependent on their scrolls. They believe they can't cast without visual reference, so they can't — the belief constrains them even though the constraint isn't real. Take away their scrolls and they're helpless, not because the magic stopped working but because they're certain it did.

The technique: using visual reference to reinforce mental clarity is actually effective. A caster who looks at a kanji while holding it in their mind's eye achieves a sharper, more precise visualization than one working from memory alone. The symbol is held simultaneously in the literal eye and the soul's eye, and the doubling strengthens the funnel. Script casters may achieve marginally better precision and consistency than equivalent casters working from pure memory, at the cost of needing their materials.

This community would have the strongest reverence for spell tomes and the deepest anxiety about preserving written knowledge. If the writing is destroyed, the magic is destroyed — that's their genuine belief. A library fire isn't a loss of reference material. It's a loss of *power itself*. They'd view the knowledge economy differently from anyone else: hoarding isn't about leverage, it's about preserving the source of all magic. Sharing isn't generosity, it's creating copies of something precious in case the original is lost.

## Shelved for Future Exploration

### The Gift

A community with a passive, grateful orientation toward magic. It arrived. They didn't earn it. They can't explain it. They accept it. Casting is receiving, not taking.

This framework would make them instinctively cautious about overcasting because the concept of *taking* from a gift feels disrespectful. Potentially one of the safest communities in the world — not from understanding, but from temperament.

**Shelved because:** The emotional framework is compelling but needs more thought on what makes this community distinct in practice beyond general caution. May overlap with the Breath or Flow communities. Revisit when a specific narrative or encounter calls for it.

## Collision and Conflict

All of these communities exist simultaneously and are roughly the same age — about a year into their understanding. When they encounter each other, their frameworks collide with the heat of recent invention. Nobody's tradition is old enough to have earned the weight of authority. Everyone is equally certain and equally new.

A Divinity community meeting an Elemancy community sees heretics — people casting without prayer, without reverence, treating Her gift as a natural phenomenon. The Elemancers see superstitious fools attributing physical effects to an imaginary deity. A Flow practitioner watching a Script caster fumble with scrolls sees someone who has completely misunderstood the nature of what they're working with. The Script caster watching the Flow practitioner cast without visual reference sees someone who shouldn't be able to do what they're doing — and might conclude they're faking it.

These collisions are where some of the richest NPC interactions live. Not combat. Not quests. Just two groups of people who discovered the same thing at the same time and arrived at completely different conclusions, each convinced the other is dangerously wrong. The player navigates between them, accumulating fragments of every framework, unable to fully endorse or dismiss any of them — because at one year in, nobody has earned the right to certainty.
