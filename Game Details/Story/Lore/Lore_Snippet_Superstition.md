# LORE SNIPPET — Superstition and the Scramble for Understanding

## The Timeline

Magic has permeated the world since the fall — two to three generations of ambient presence, saturating the land, amplifying life, reshaping ecosystems. But for most of that time, humans didn't know they could interact with it directly. Magic was just the way the world was. Plants grew differently. Animals were more dangerous. The land had moods and rhythms that the old world never had. People adapted to living in a magical environment without understanding that they could *use* it.

The discovery that humans can channel magic through kanji is roughly a year old.

One year. That's how long anyone has had to figure out how any of this works. There are no experts. There are no traditions. There are no schools of thought refined over decades of practice. There are people who discovered something extraordinary twelve months ago and have been making sense of it in real time, with no framework, no teachers, no shared knowledge base, and no margin for error in a world that was already dangerous.

## The First Year

The discovery likely happened independently in multiple communities around the same time — different people, different places, different kanji, arriving at the same realization through different paths. Someone focused on an old-world symbol and something happened. They tried again. It happened again. They showed someone else. Word spread locally. Each community began experimenting with whatever symbols they had access to, whatever old-world artifacts they'd preserved or scavenged.

A year is enough time for some basic spells to be discovered and shared within local communities. Enough time for a few lucky or clever people to stumble onto pairings. Enough time for people to start getting hurt — casting too much, draining the local mana without understanding what was happening, drawing from themselves without realizing the cost. Enough time for the first hollowing cases to appear, terrifying and inexplicable, in communities that pushed too hard too fast.

The village attackers — the corrupted beings that assault the protagonist's home — may be among the very first hollowing cases anyone has ever seen. Not the result of a long slide into corruption. The result of desperate people who discovered magic, leaned on it too hard to survive, and broke themselves in a matter of months. Nobody in the protagonist's village has a framework for understanding what happened to them. They're just monsters that came from the dark. The understanding that they were once people, that the hollowing made them this way, that the same thing could happen to anyone — that understanding doesn't exist yet. It has to be built from scratch.

## The Superstition Layer

When people discover something powerful and have no framework to explain it, they build one from whatever's available: observation, coincidence, fear, hope, and pattern-matching against incomplete data.

Every community that has discovered magic is developing its own explanations in real time. These aren't ancient traditions or inherited folk wisdom. They're fresh, unstable, desperate interpretations being invented month by month by people who need *some* explanation for what's happening around them. The beliefs are held with the intensity of recent converts, not the quiet certainty of ancestral practice. People are scared. People are excited. People are clinging to whatever seems to work.

**Casting rituals.** A community where the first person to successfully cast spoke the kanji aloud now believes vocalization is necessary. A community where the first caster traced the symbol in the air before visualizing it has built that gesture into their practice. A community where someone cast successfully while holding a specific object now treats that object as essential. None of these are mechanically necessary — the funnel works through mental visualization alone. But each community's first experience shaped their understanding, and nobody has enough data to separate the essential from the incidental. The ritual and the result are fused in their minds because they've never seen one without the other.

**Mana beliefs.** People can feel that magic is easier in some places than others. They can feel when an area is drained, even if they don't know the word for it. From this basic sensory data, a wild variety of explanations have sprung up in the span of months. A group that keeps living plants on their bodies because they believe it strengthens their magic — probably negligible in actual effect, but not zero, and if the confidence helps with mental clarity, the practice reinforces itself. A settlement that sings together before casting because they noticed magic felt easier afterward — wrong about why, but a large group of emotionally present living people in one place might marginally boost the ambient pool. A village that forbids casting at night because their first bad experience happened after dark. Each belief is brand new, fiercely held, and untested against alternatives.

**Corruption myths.** The hollowing is so new that most communities haven't even seen it yet. Those that have are terrified and grasping for explanations. One group might believe it's a punishment from the land. Another might think corrupted creatures carry it like a disease. A third might believe it only happens to people who cast alone, because the one case they witnessed involved a solitary individual. A fourth might not believe it exists at all — they haven't seen it, so travelers' warnings sound like paranoid nonsense. There is no consensus because there hasn't been time for one to form. Every community is working from a sample size of zero, one, or two cases at most.

**Protective practices.** Things people do to ward off danger that have no mechanical basis but feel necessary because the alternative is doing nothing in the face of the unknown. Wearing certain colors. Eating before casting. Never casting the same spell twice in a row. Burying your spell tome at night. These practices are weeks or months old at most. They originated from single observations generalized into rules — someone did X and something bad happened, so don't do X. Someone did Y and something good happened, so always do Y. The human need for control in the face of the incomprehensible, expressed through brand new rituals that already feel ancient to the people practicing them because a year of constant fear stretches long.

## The Accuracy Spectrum

Some superstitions are pure noise. Don't cast at night. Face east. Bury your tome. These are coincidence hardened into conviction. They do nothing. They feel essential.

Some are wrong in reasoning but right in outcome. The group that believes casting alone is dangerous has no idea why — but casting without someone to tell you to stop genuinely is riskier. They've arrived at a sound conclusion through faulty logic, and the conclusion will protect them regardless.

Some are almost touching the real system without knowing it. The plant-wearers. The singers. The community that insists on resting between casting sessions because they believe the land needs time to "wake up" — they don't know about mana pools or regeneration rates, but they've independently invented a practice that respects exactly the right principle.

And some may be touching something real that even the researcher and mentor haven't figured out yet. A community's seemingly absurd belief might contain an observation that, properly understood, reveals something genuine about how magic works. The signal is buried in noise, and at one year in, nobody — not even the most rigorous minds in the world — can be sure which is which.

**The player should never be entirely sure either.** The ambiguity persists throughout the game. Some beliefs will be clearly wrong. Some will be obviously sensible. And a meaningful number should sit in the space where the player genuinely can't tell — where the superstition might be nonsense, or might be the seed of something the researcher would kill to understand.

## The Researcher and Mentor in Context

The researcher and mentor are remarkable not because they know a lot, but because they're approaching this methodically when everyone else is grasping. In a world where every community is running on instinct and fresh superstition, these two are trying to be rigorous. Testing hypotheses. Recording results. Controlling variables where possible. Building a structured body of work from first principles in a field that didn't exist a year ago.

This makes their collaborative project both more impressive and more fragile than it might seem. A year of careful, structured observation makes them possibly the most informed people in the world — and they still barely understand what they're dealing with. Their notes aren't a mature reference library. They're the frantic, meticulous beginnings of a discipline that doesn't have a name yet. Every finding is provisional. Every conclusion is held lightly, ready to be revised by tomorrow's data. The mentor's caution about sharing isn't "we've verified this is dangerous." It's "we don't know enough to be sure this is safe."

The brother's impatience with this caution makes more sense in this context too. A year in, people are dying. Communities are struggling. Corrupted beings are appearing. And the two smartest people the brother knows are saying "we need more time." His response — let me go get you the data faster, let me push into the places you're afraid to go, let me accelerate this — isn't recklessness. It's urgency. Real, justified urgency that happens to be the thing that destroys him.

## Competing Beliefs in Real Time

When two communities meet — which is rare given the isolation of the post-fall world, but happens through trade, wandering, and necessity — their magical cultures collide. Not as ancient traditions meeting across centuries of divergence, but as fresh theories clashing with the heat of recent invention. Both sides discovered magic around the same time. Both sides have been experimenting for roughly a year. Both sides are certain their approach is correct because it's the only approach they've ever known.

These collisions are volatile. A community that casts silently meeting one that chants. A group that rests between sessions meeting one that casts continuously. A settlement that fears the hollowing meeting one that doesn't believe it's real. Each looks at the other and sees ignorance, recklessness, or superstition — without recognizing that their own certainty is built on the same thin foundation.

The player, moving between communities, encounters this constantly. Every settlement has a different version of truth. Some contradict each other directly. Some overlap in unexpected ways. The player accumulates fragments of understanding from every source, and the challenge isn't finding the right answer — it's recognizing that nobody has it yet. Not the villages. Not the researcher. Not the player. The world is one year into the most profound transformation in human history, and everyone is guessing. Some are guessing better than others. Nobody is done.
