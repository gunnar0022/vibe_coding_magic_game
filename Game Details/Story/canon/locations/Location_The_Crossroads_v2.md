# The Crossroads

## Locked Endpoints

- No large hub town exists — that would contradict the lore of isolated, post-collapse communities.
- The crossroads is not a settlement. Nobody lives here. There are no houses, no permanent structures beyond pre-collapse remnants.
- It exists because geography makes it inevitable — a river crossing where paths between four neighboring villages converge.
- People camp here because it's convenient, not because anyone organized a gathering.
- Trade happens as a byproduct of overlap, not as a purpose.

## Narrative Function

- The brother passes through because everyone does eventually.
- The farmer trades surplus here. The researcher compares notes with curious people from other villages. The wanderer rests between legs of a longer journey.
- Same place, different reasons, different NPCs foregrounded per path.
- Preserves the isolation of individual communities while giving paths a natural point of intersection.
- Not a safe haven. Not governed. Not even really a destination. Just geography forcing people to overlap.

---

## The Four Villages

The crossroads sits in the space between four communities. All of them are small — the player's home village of roughly fifty to sixty people is on the smaller end, while others might have over a hundred. They're similar in culture and sophistication. None of them have developed grand interpretations of magic. They all landed on roughly the same pragmatic understanding: magic is what it is, some symbols seem to do things, nobody really knows why.

The villages are vaguely aware of each other. They're loosely allied in the sense that they don't threaten each other and occasionally help when asked, but contact is infrequent and there's no shared governance, no formal agreements, no treaties. Each village handles its own affairs. They are neighbors in the geographic sense, not the social one.

Most people in each village have never been to the crossroads and never will. They stay home, work the fields, raise families, and hear about the other villages secondhand from whoever made the trip last. The crossroads is a place for gatherers, craftspeople with portable skills, and the occasionally curious. Not for whole communities.

## Location

**Name:** The Crossroads. No local name — everyone calls it by its function.

**Geography:** A river crossing in the foothills where three valley routes converge. The river is wide and swift — unfordable except here, where an old stone bridge spans it. Pre-collapse construction, still standing. The bridge is the centerpiece and the reason this spot matters. Without it, the river would be an impassable barrier. With it, paths from all four villages' territories naturally funnel together.

The crossing sits in a natural amphitheater of low hills. Open ground. Enough space for a dozen tents. Not enough to sustain any kind of permanent habitation — the ground is rocky and poor for farming, the water source runs seasonal, and winter cold would be brutal to endure without real shelter.

## Physical Character

**Most days:** Empty. The bridge stands alone. Wind moves through the hills. The river sounds constant. A few semi-permanent setups dot the area — a tarp staked over a workbench, a stone-ringed fire pit that someone maintains, a lean-to built against a boulder that's been repaired enough times to be almost permanent. These belong to people like Meryl — craftspeople or regular visitors who keep a presence here because it's convenient, but whose real homes are in their villages.

**On busy days:** A handful of tents. Five, eight, maybe ten people from two or three villages. Gatherers who camped here because the best foraging grounds are closer to the crossroads than to home. A craftsman repairing tools in exchange for food. Someone who came specifically to find someone else. Fires at night. Quiet conversation. The feeling of a campsite, not a marketplace.

**At its busiest — certain seasons when gathering peaks:** Maybe fifteen to twenty people over the course of a week, staggered arrivals and departures. This is as crowded as it ever gets, and even at peak, it feels like a busy campground, not a bazaar. People arrive, trade what they have, share news, and leave within a few days.

**Aesthetics:**
- The stone bridge is beautiful in a functional way. Weathered. Moss and lichen on the northern face. The blocks are fitted with a precision that speaks of the old world. It's the most impressive piece of construction most of these people have ever seen, and they walk across it without thinking about it.
- The water below is clear and fast, loud. The sound is constant — a baseline hum that fills every silence.
- The hills are bare or sparse vegetation. Wind-scoured, not forest. Open sightlines in every direction. You can see someone approaching from a long way off, which is part of why this place feels safe enough to camp at. No surprises.
- Woodsmoke when someone's here. Silence when they're not.

## How It Functions

Nobody runs the crossroads. Nobody governs it, schedules it, or maintains it beyond the individuals who keep their own semi-permanent spots in order. It exists because geography makes it inevitable, and it continues to exist because the pattern is useful.

**Why people come here:**

Gatherers from all four villages range into the surrounding wilderness to collect food, herbs, materials — whatever the amplified wild provides. The gathering grounds shift seasonally, and sometimes the best foraging is closer to the crossroads than to home. Rather than haul a full load back to the village and make a separate trip to trade, a gatherer camps at the crossroads for a few days. While they're there, a gatherer from another village is often doing the same thing for the same reason.

Trade happens because of this overlap. Not organized, not formal. You're already here. Someone else is already here. You have surplus dried meat, they have surplus grain. You swap. Maybe you also swap news — who's seen what, where the gathering's been good, what areas feel wrong or depleted. Then you pack up and go home.

**Barter only.** There is no currency. About a decade ago, someone clever tried to establish a token system — a form of banking that would function only at the crossroads, with exchangeable markers representing goods. It collapsed within a season. People didn't trust it, enforcement was impossible, and when a few people lost value in the system, trust evaporated entirely. The memory of that failure lingers. Anyone who suggests formalizing trade at the crossroads gets a tired look and a story about the time someone already tried.

**Semi-permanent presences.** A few people from the surrounding villages maintain setups here — Meryl's workbench, a healer's sheltered spot, a gatherer who's made the crossroads her effective base camp because she ranges so far that no single village is really home anymore. These people aren't residents. They have homes elsewhere. But they're here often enough that other visitors expect to find them, and that reliability is itself a form of value.

**Disputes** are rare because the population is small and everyone is loosely known to each other through overlapping village connections. When conflicts happen, they're handled between the people involved. If someone behaves badly enough — theft, violence, cheating a trade — word gets back to their village, and the social consequence follows them home. The crossroads doesn't need law because the communities behind it provide accountability at a distance.

## The Research Anchor

In the past year, since magic became accessible, the crossroads has gained a second function beyond practical trade: it's where people compare notes.

Discovery through resonance is personal, scattered, and unpredictable. A farmer in one village feels 土 during a moment of deep connection with the land. A craftsman in another village feels 鉄 while working at his forge. A fighter in a third village discovers 火 in a moment of rage. Each person's experience is isolated — they have no way of knowing that other people in other villages are having similar experiences with different symbols.

The crossroads is the one place where these reports converge. Gatherers who've had resonance experiences mention them to other gatherers. People who've heard about a strange mark on a lightning-struck tree pass the story along. Over a few months, the crossroads has become the place where you go if you felt something strange and want to know if anyone else did too.

This isn't organized. There's no research institution, no scholar's camp, no formal exchange. It's just conversation — campfire talk between people from different villages who are comparing fragments of an experience none of them fully understand. "I felt a symbol appear in my mind last month." "My cousin found a mark on a tree stump after a storm." "Has anyone else tried holding two symbols at once?"

The companion researcher is drawn here because they recognized, before most people, that these scattered reports are pieces of a larger picture. They're not at the crossroads to excavate ruins or study artifacts. They're here to listen. To collect accounts from different villages, document what people have experienced, and look for patterns. They're the person who shows up with a notebook and genuine curiosity and asks the questions nobody else thinks to ask: "When you felt the symbol arrive, what were you doing? What were you feeling? Where were you standing?"

The researcher stands out at the crossroads not because they're powerful or prestigious, but because they're *systematic* in a place where everyone else is anecdotal. They're building something from fragments that other people treat as isolated curiosities. That's how the player encounters them — someone crouched by a fire, notebook open, deep in conversation with a gatherer who's describing something strange that happened near the north ridge.

## Specific Encounters Per Path

### Knowledge Path
- The companion researcher is here, collecting accounts and comparing notes from different villages.
- The researcher knows that the mentor — their teacher, based elsewhere — would want to hear what's being reported at the crossroads. The crossroads is their field site. The mentor's location is the laboratory.
- The player encounters other curious people — not scholars, just villagers who felt something and came looking for answers. The conversations are messy, contradictory, and occasionally illuminating.

### Power Path
- The brother may have passed through recently, asking sharp questions about threats, corrupted sightings, areas to avoid or seek out.
- Other capable fighters or hunters from neighboring villages pass through. Not mercenaries — just people who've taken on similar roles in their own communities.
- Information about dangerous areas, unusual creatures, and depleted zones flows through the crossroads as practical warnings between villages.

### Community Path
- The crossroads is where the village trades its surplus for what it can't produce.
- The player might come here as the village's representative, carrying goods and bringing back what's needed.
- Senna might send the player specifically because she trusts them to negotiate fairly and bring back what the village actually needs.
- Encounters with craftspeople and farmers from other villages reinforce the community path's themes — cooperation, mutual aid, the practical work of survival.

### Wandering Path
- The crossroads is a waypoint, not a destination. A place to rest, resupply, and move on.
- The wanderer-mentor might be found here occasionally — they don't follow any schedule or belong to any village, so their presence is unpredictable.
- The player encounters other drifters, people in transit, people who don't quite fit in their home communities. The crossroads is the one place where not-belonging is normal.

### All Paths
- **The brother passes through** at some point, regardless of path. His presence is brief and memorable. He doesn't stay. He resupplies, asks pointed questions, and moves on. On active paths, the player might encounter him here directly. On passive paths, the player hears about him secondhand — "An intense young man came through. Asked about corrupted creatures. Didn't sleep. Left before dawn."
- **Meryl is here.** Not every time, but often enough. His workbench, his tools, his quiet competence. The player's first encounter with him — a minor repair job, a practical interaction — happens naturally because this is where practical people overlap.
