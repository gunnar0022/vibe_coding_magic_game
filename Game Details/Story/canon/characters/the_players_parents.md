# The Player's Parents

## Locked Endpoints

- Die during the coordinated village attack by hollowed creatures.
- They must be SPECIFIC PEOPLE — not narrative fuel, not archetypes.
- The player should remember something about each of them.
- The brother was proud of the praise he received from them — they validated his role in the family and village.

## Known Relationships

- **The Player** — their child. The loss defines the game's emotional foundation.
- **The Brother** — their older child. His pride came from their approval. Their death transforms his personality entirely.

## Development Requirements (from master doc)

The prologue must include interactions with both parents that establish them as individuals. The player needs enough time with them (~20-30 min tutorial) to feel the coming loss. What the player remembers about them should make the grief specific, not generic.

---

## Names & Roles

**Thorne** (the father) — Village carpenter and builder. Quiet. Works with his hands. Has repaired and maintained nearly every structure in the village at some point. Knows the practical, structural heart of everything that exists.

**Elsa** (the mother) — Midwife, herbalist, and keeper of stores. Self-taught through experience and observation—there's no "formal healing" in a post-collapse world. She tends wounds with practical knowledge (herbs, poultices, rest). Delivers babies. Knows which plants help which ailments. The village goes to her when things break, but she's figuring it out as she goes, like everyone else. She's experimenting with how magic might help healing, but it's tentative, uncertain work.

## Character Texture

**Thorne** is the kind of quiet that comes from confidence in skill, not from shyness. He doesn't talk much, but when he does, he's direct. He notices small failures — a beam that's starting to rot, a corner that's settling wrong — and fixes them before anyone else sees the problem. The village is solid because Thorne pays attention. He takes pride in invisible work.

The brother loves him because Thorne *approves quietly*. When the brother learns a new technique, makes a tool better, or demonstrates competence, Thorne notices. Not with fanfare. With a nod. With "That's good work." The kind of validation that means *something* because it comes from someone whose standards are high and whose praise is scarce. The brother has internalized Thorne's values: competence is virtue. Excellence matters. Acknowledge it when you see it, but don't make a spectacle of it.

**Elsa** is warm without being sentimental. She jokes dryly, mostly to herself, while she works. She remembers every person she's healed and what they were like in pain — angry or quiet or afraid. This knowledge of human weakness makes her patient. She doesn't judge people for breaking. She's seen them all break. She helps them reassemble.

She validated the brother differently than Thorne did — she would ask him to help her work (holding patients still during difficult births, learning which symptoms match which remedies), and in the doing, she trusted him. Trust is the form of love she spoke in. The brother felt that, even if he didn't articulate it that way.

Both parents valued *contribution*. They didn't raise a son. They raised someone who pulled his weight, who noticed what needed doing, and who did it. The brother became exactly that. And that's why their absence breaks something structural in him.

## The Evening Before — A Scene That Shows Who They Are

**Setting:** The village central plaza, evening. The sun is low. People are finishing their day's work. There's a harvest meal being prepared — nothing fancy, just the village gathering together for food.

**The Player** walks through the plaza carrying water. One of their small tasks for the evening.

**Thorne** is at the far edge, working on the framework of a new grain storage shelter. It's taking shape — walls up, roof beams being fitted. The work is nearly done, but he's inspecting a joint, turning it in his hands, considering. The brother is there too, holding a beam while Thorne works.

The Player approaches their father. "How's it coming?"

"Well enough," Thorne says, not looking away from the joint. "This one went in clean." He hands it to the brother. "That corner there. Check how it fits."

The brother takes it, moves to position it. It slides into place with a satisfying click.

Thorne looks at it. Nods. "That's good work. You remembered the angle from the eastern storage."

It's a small thing. One line of praise. But the way the brother straightens slightly — you can see the weight of that approval land in his shoulders. Thorne notices things. Thorne doesn't say things he doesn't mean.

The Player sets down the water and helps hold a beam while Thorne works on the next joint. Their hands fit where their father guides them. No instruction, just: *do this, help me with this*. The work is how he shows them they belong in it.

After a while, **Elsa** comes through the plaza with a basket. She's been gathering evening rounds — checking on old Vera who had a cough, making sure the woman who gave birth last month isn't running fever. She sees them working and pulls the brother aside briefly.

"Walk with me for a moment," she says. He does.

They move out of earshot. The Player can't hear the words, but they see Elsa hold up a jar she's been carrying. She's talking, nodding toward the medical supplies. The brother listens, then nods back. He takes the jar — he's going to carry it somewhere for her, bring it to someone, be useful in the way she's asking. It's not a request. It's an assumption of his reliability.

When he comes back to help with the beam, his step is steadier. Both parents saw him today. Both assumed he was someone who could handle something real.

## Three Elements This Scene Accomplishes

1. **Shows the parents as specific people with specific roles and ways of being.** Thorne is quiet competence. Elsa is trusting work. The brother learns his values from them. When they die, he loses the people who shaped him and the only people whose judgment of his competence actually mattered.

2. **Establishes why the brother can't stay after the attack.** The village becomes a place where his parents aren't. Every shelter Thorne built is still standing — a rebuke. Every person Elsa healed is still walking around — a reminder. The loss is *structural*. He can't rebuild something his father already built. He can't be the helpful younger sibling in a village full of people his mother would have wanted to help.

3. **Plants the seed for the brother's corruption arc.** The brother internalizes: "Excellence matters. Competence is virtue. Do the work. Fix what's broken." This becomes the foundation of his obsession. When he's hollowing himself out, he's not thinking "*I'm taking from myself.*" He's thinking "*The world is broken. I have to fix it.*" His parents' values become the justification for his self-destruction.

## Death Endpoints (Not Scenes — Just Notes)

Both parents die during the coordinated assault. The Player witnesses at least one death. The specificity comes from how their character defines their death:

- **Thorne** dies protecting a building or defending someone structurally important (like the children). He makes a choice consistent with his values.
- **Elsa** dies helping someone in need — possibly the Player or the brother, or someone in the village who needed healing. She doesn't run. She stays where she's needed.

The brother saves the Player's life. He's not with the parents when they die. This is intentional — he carries the guilt of not being there, not being able to save them, not being able to protect the people he loved most.
