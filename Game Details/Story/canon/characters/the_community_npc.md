# SENNA — Community Path Anchor

## Core Role

Senna is the reason the player stays. Not through argument — through need. She's the face of a village that's lost its center and is looking to whoever's still standing. Her pitch isn't a sales job. It's a reality: if you leave, who's left?

---

## Who She Was Before

Senna is close to the player's age — old enough to carry real responsibility, young enough that she shouldn't have to carry this much. She grew up in the village alongside the player, the brother, and Kael. Not part of their tight circle in the same way, but present. Familiar. The person who was always nearby, always involved, always helping someone.

Her father was the closest thing the village had to a chief. Not a title anyone voted on — just the person everyone looked to. He kept the food stocks balanced. He checked in on families to make sure labor was distributed fairly. He mediated squabbles before they became feuds. He noticed when someone was struggling and quietly redirected help their way. The kind of leadership that's invisible when it's working and catastrophic when it's gone.

Senna was his shadow. She'd been helping him since she was old enough to carry a message across the village. By her teens, she was doing half the work — tracking who needed what, minding the younger children while their parents worked the fields, relaying her father's decisions when he was busy elsewhere. She knew every family's situation. She knew which kids hadn't eaten. She knew which neighbors weren't speaking and why. Not because she was ambitious or political. Because her father did this, and she loved her father, and the work felt natural.

She was the older sister the whole village shared. Not a mother figure exactly — too young for that, too close in age to the children she watched. But the one who'd crouch down to a crying kid's eye level and stay there until the crying stopped. The one who remembered everyone's name, everyone's problem, everyone's birthday. Reliable in the way that people take for granted until it's gone.

She had no children of her own. She didn't need any. She had the whole village.

---

## What She Lost

Her father died in the attack. He died doing what he always did — trying to organize, trying to protect, trying to hold things together while they fell apart. He wasn't a fighter. He was a man who walked toward the chaos because someone needed to tell people where to go.

Senna wasn't with him when it happened. She was across the village, pulling children into shelter, counting heads, doing exactly what her father had trained her to do in a crisis — protect the ones who can't protect themselves. She did everything right. She saved lives. And while she was saving them, her father was killed.

She didn't get to say goodbye. She didn't get to be there. She was doing his work in one part of the village while he died doing his work in another.

---

## What Happened After

The village looked at her. That's all it took.

Nobody appointed her. Nobody asked. Her father was gone, and the village needed someone who knew how things worked — who tracked the food, who mediated the disputes, who made sure the labor was balanced and the children were fed. Senna was the only person who knew all of it because she'd been doing half of it for years.

So she stepped into it. Not with confidence. Not with a sense of rising to the occasion. With the nauseating feeling of putting on shoes that don't fit and walking anyway because people are watching and someone has to move first.

She's filling her father's role because nobody else can, and she knows — with a certainty she can't say out loud — that she's not him. He did this with warmth, with instinct, with an ease that made it look effortless. She does it with lists in her head and a tightness in her chest and the constant low-grade terror that she's going to miss something he wouldn't have missed. That someone will go hungry because she forgot. That a conflict will spiral because she didn't see it coming the way he always did.

The village doesn't see this. The village sees someone who stepped up. Who's been tireless since the attack. Who's everywhere — with the children, with the grieving, with the rebuilding, with the food distribution, with every decision that needs making. They see strength. They see her father's daughter.

What they don't see is that the work is the only thing between Senna and the grief underneath. She hasn't processed her father's death. She hasn't sat with it. She hasn't had a single unoccupied hour since the attack because every unoccupied hour is an hour where the weight drops and she's not sure she can carry it. The work isn't healing her. It's holding her together the way a bandage holds together a wound that hasn't been cleaned. Functional. Temporary. Dangerous if it goes on too long.

---

## Her Relationship to the Player

Senna and the player share a history — they grew up in the same village, know the same people, lost the same things. But their dynamic now is shaped by an asymmetry: Senna stayed. She's been here, every day, doing the work. The player has been grieving too, but the player still has a choice ahead of them. Senna's choice is already made. She can't leave. The village won't let her, and she won't let herself.

When the brother announces he's leaving, Senna feels it like a second attack. Another person walking away. Another absence the village has to absorb. She doesn't blame him — or she tries not to. But the fear underneath is raw: if people keep leaving, what's left?

Her appeal to the player isn't a speech. It's not calculated. It's the accumulated weight of everything she's been holding, slipping out in a quiet moment.

*"I can't do this alone. I know you need to make your own choice. But I need you to know that if you go, I'm still here. And it's getting harder."*

She doesn't say "you owe the village." She doesn't guilt the player explicitly. She just tells the truth: she's drowning, slowly, and she's too proud and too needed to say it to anyone except someone she trusts. The player is one of the few people left who knew her before — before the attack, before the role, before the weight. With the player, she can be the person she actually is rather than the person the village needs her to be.

That vulnerability is her real pull. Not obligation. Not duty. The simple human fact that someone you've known your whole life is asking for help and means it.

---

## If the Player Stays (Community Path)

The community path isn't Senna giving the player orders. It's two people sharing a burden that was designed for someone older and wiser than either of them.

The player becomes Senna's partner in the work — not her subordinate, not her replacement, but the person who takes half the weight so she can breathe. Food distribution becomes manageable. Rebuilding accelerates. The children have another familiar face. Disputes have two mediators instead of one exhausted person trying to channel her dead father's instincts.

Over time, Senna starts to unclench. Not all at once. Not dramatically. But the player's presence creates space — physical space in the workload, emotional space in the grief. She starts to say things about her father. Small things at first. How he used to handle a particular family's complaints. A habit he had. Something he said once that she didn't understand until now.

The player who stays watches Senna go from someone surviving to someone living. It's slow. It's not linear. There are bad days. But the trajectory is real, and the player's presence is the reason it's possible.

This is the community path's emotional core: not building walls and growing crops, but being present for someone who needed you to stay, and finding that staying built something worth having.

---

## If the Player Leaves

Senna doesn't stop. She can't. The village still needs someone, and she's still the only one who knows how everything fits together.

But the weight doesn't distribute. It concentrates. The player's absence is one more thing Senna absorbs without complaint, one more gap she fills by working harder, sleeping less, sitting with her grief even less than before. The village survives — Senna makes sure of it — but Senna herself erodes.

On active paths, the player might hear about the village through travelers. "That settlement with the young woman running everything. She looks tired." On the wandering path, the player might return to find a village that functions but feels brittle — held together by someone who has been holding too tight for too long.

Senna won't say she's angry. She might not even be angry. But there will be something in her eyes when the player returns — a distance that wasn't there before. Not resentment. Just the recognition that the player made a choice, and Senna lived with the consequences.

---

## Design Notes

**Senna is not a guilt trip.** Her appeal works because it's genuine, not because it's manipulative. The player who chooses to leave isn't punished by the narrative — they're simply shown what their absence costs someone specific. The player who stays isn't rewarded with praise — they're given the quiet, grinding, meaningful work of helping someone carry a weight that's too heavy for one person.

**The father's absence is the wound, not the attack.** The attack killed people and destroyed buildings. But for Senna, the real devastation is the specific absence of the person who held everything together and the impossible task of replacing him. The attack is the event. The father's death is the loss. The difference matters.

**Senna's work-as-suppression mirrors the brother's journey-as-suppression.** Both are coping with loss by refusing to stop moving. The brother runs outward. Senna runs in place. Neither is processing. Neither is healing. Both are using purpose to avoid grief. The parallel should be visible to a player paying attention — these two people responded to the same catastrophe with the same strategy expressed in opposite directions.

**The older sister archetype, not the mother.** Senna is too young to be maternal about this. She's not nurturing the village from a position of wisdom. She's holding it together with the same tools she used to help her father — organization, attention, care — but without his steady hand guiding hers. She's an older sibling doing a parent's job. The distinction keeps her sympathetic rather than saintly.


## Senna's Emotional Arc

### Act 1 (Prologue/Early Game)
- Desperate, exhausted, holding on
- Grieving Kira fresh enough that it shapes every interaction
- The pitch to the player is a last act of hope

### Act 2 (Mid-Game)
- If the player stays: Gradually less desperate, more grounded, finding rhythm in rebuilding
- Realizing the player is choosing to stay, which changes everything
- Releasing the grip on obligation

### Act 3 (Late Game)
- Healed enough to be real. Still grieving (always will be), but not broken by it.
- Standing with the player at endgame as a peer, not a suppliant
- Ready to build something new, not just survive what's left
