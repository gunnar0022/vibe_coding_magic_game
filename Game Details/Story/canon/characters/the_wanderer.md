# The Wanderer / Mentor (Wandering/Detachment Path)

**Name:** RAHN

## Locked Endpoints

- NOT a walking companion. A presence found at destinations.
- Someone the player encounters at places they arrive, not someone who walks alongside them.
- A recurring figure who shares wisdom about their own journey.
- Has lost everything already. Found peace in movement.

## NPC Summary Line

"Hard-won peace bought by abandoning everyone who needed them. Right about the danger, absent for the fight."

## Established Traits

- Calm where everyone else is frantic.
- Has seen what the other paths lead to.
- Advises nonviolence and disconnection with quiet certainty of someone who's been through it.
- From their detached perspective, even losing the brother is inevitable — "the way the wind blows."
- Cryptic advice grounded in real experience, not mysticism.

## Flaw

- Has stopped caring enough to act. Their peace was bought by abandoning responsibility.
- Being right doesn't help the people who needed them.

## What the Player Finds (Wandering Path)

- The world is genuinely beautiful. Freedom, serenity, the most exploration of any path.
- The question is whether beauty is enough when the people you loved are still out there breaking.

---

## Character Identity

**Name:** RAHN (pronounced "RAH-hn")

A person in their late forties or early fifties. Difficult to read age precisely—weathered, but not worn. They move with the ease of someone who's walked for a very long time. Eyes are clear and observant. They carry almost nothing: a sparse bedroll, a small pouch of personal items (a flat stone, a dried flower, a sketch on bark), a water vessel. No spell tome, no weapons. They've let those go.

---

## The Spine: From Attached to Detached

### Origin Point (Approximately 20 Years Ago)

Rahn grew up in a coastal settlement. Fishing community, tight-knit, about twenty families. They had a role: keeper of navigational knowledge, map-maker, story-holder. They were good at it. People relied on them.

They had someone they loved. A partner (name: Ven). They shared a dwelling at the edge of the settlement, overlooking the water. Small life, but deep.

### The Turning Point (Approximately 15 Years Ago)

The settlement's fishing grounds began failing. Not from magic—magic wasn't something anyone knew about yet. The currents shifted. The fish moved to deeper water. The coast eroded in storms. The community that had sustained itself for decades faced slow starvation.

Rahn saw the trajectory early. Advised the community to move inland, find new resources. But moving meant abandoning the settlement, the structures, the history. People refused. They wanted to fix what was broken, not leave it.

Rahn pushed harder. Other community members pushed back. "We're not leaving our home." "We can solve this if we work together." "You're abandoning us if you go."

Rahn stayed. Tried to help. For months, worked alongside the community on solutions: new fishing methods, inland foraging, rationing. Some things helped. Nothing was enough. The community grew more desperate. People pushed their bodies harder, ate less, worked longer. Some collapsed from exhaustion. Some got sick and didn't recover.

Ven was among those who refused to stop. Not dramatically, but persistently. Taking on extra work. Eating last. Going out in weather that should have kept everyone inside. "The community needs this." "People depend on me." "If I don't do this, who will?"

Rahn begged them to stop. "You're wearing yourself away. Please."

But Ven couldn't stop. The need to contribute was stronger than the need to survive.

### The Breaking Point

Ven died. Not suddenly—gradually. An illness that a rested body might have fought off, but Ven's body had nothing left to fight with. Rahn watched them fade over weeks, unable to do anything except be present for the slow diminishing of someone they loved.

At some point—Rahn can never be certain exactly when—the person in that bed stopped being Ven and became just a body waiting to finish.

Rahn's choice after Ven died: stay in a community that had cost them everything, or leave.

They left.

### The Wandering (Following 15 Years)

Rahn walked away from the coastal settlement. Alone. Never looked back. Not from coldness, but from necessity. Looking back would mean drowning.

They wandered. Traveled to different settlements, different regions, different communities. They carried nothing but themselves and their grief and the knowledge that they couldn't have changed anything.

Along the way, they encountered other communities making the same mistake in different forms. Different resources, different circumstances, same pattern: people facing scarcity, refusing to accept limitation, destroying themselves through obligation and desperation. A settlement that overhunted until the game fled. A community that cut every tree for fuel and then had nothing to build with. People who worked until they broke because stopping felt like giving up.

Eventually, Rahn stopped trying to prevent it or fix it. They simply *saw* it. Observed it. Accepted it as the way humans move through the world—always taking more than they can sustain, always sacrificing themselves for something they believe is bigger than themselves.

They found peace in that acceptance.

But the peace came at a cost: they became unable to act. Unable to intervene. Unable to care enough to stop it. They became wise about the pattern but absent for the fight.

### The New Horror (This Past Year)

About a year ago, something changed in the world.

Rahn felt it before they understood it—a shift in the quality of air, the way water moved, the way certain places hummed with something that had always been there but was suddenly louder. People everywhere began discovering they could *do* things. Channel something through symbols. Shape the world with intention.

Rahn approached this the way they approach everything now: with quiet attention and no urgency. They observed. They took what the world offered—small, humble uses of mana, the way you'd take a berry from a tree. Not all of it. Not greedily. Just enough to understand what it was.

But others didn't approach it that way.

In the months since magic became usable, Rahn has watched communities fall into the same pattern they've seen a hundred times—except now the mechanism is more terrible than anything they've witnessed before. People aren't just working themselves to exhaustion. They're *drawing from themselves*. Carving out pieces of who they are to fuel magic they barely understand. And the void that opens up fills with something raw and singular.

Rahn has seen it happen twice now. Maybe three times. Not over years—over *months*. A settlement where people began channeling without understanding the cost. A wanderer who pushed too hard and became something that barely remembered being human. Each time, the same pattern Rahn has watched their whole life: people facing scarcity, refusing to accept limitation, destroying themselves. But now the destruction is literal. Now it has a name that people are starting to whisper: *hollowing*.

The hollowing terrifies Rahn in a way nothing has since Ven died. Because it's the same pattern—obligation becoming self-destruction—made visible and irreversible. When Ven worked themselves to death, it was tragic but comprehensible. When a person hollows, they don't just die. They become something else. Something that keeps moving with only one drive left inside it.

Rahn recognizes the pattern. Not because they've seen hollowing before—no one has, not really, not until this year. But because the *human* pattern underneath it is ancient. People have always done this. Magic just makes the cost more terrible.

## What Rahn Knows

### About Human Desperation (A Lifetime of Observation)

Before magic, Rahn had already watched the cycle repeat in a hundred forms:
- Communities that overfished until the fish vanished
- Settlements that burned every tree and then couldn't rebuild
- People who worked themselves to death because stopping felt like surrender
- The pattern was always the same: scarcity, desperation, refusal to accept limitation, self-destruction

Rahn saw Ven make that choice in the simplest possible form: keep giving everything to the community, or accept that survival means pulling back. Ven chose to keep giving. Eventually there was nothing left to give.

### About Hollowing (New and Terrible)

In the last year, Rahn has watched magic make the old pattern *literal*. People aren't just exhausting themselves—they're carving pieces of themselves away to fuel something they don't understand. The mechanism is new. The human drive underneath it is ancient.

What Rahn understands about hollowing (from a few months of careful observation):
- It's not a disease that strikes randomly—it's the same old choice, made under pressure, made repeatedly until it becomes irreversible
- It's not corruption in the sense of outside darkness entering—it's internal void organizing around what the person already was
- The endpoint is a being that is no longer the person they were, but is operating on their own emotional logic
- The person can stop it any time, but stopping means accepting loss and limitation
- Most people can't accept that. They keep going

This isn't wisdom earned from decades of watching hollowing. It's wisdom earned from decades of watching humans, applied to the most devastating iteration of the pattern Rahn has ever seen.

### About the Brother's Specific Path

Rahn doesn't know the brother's name or story directly, but they can read the signs in the player. In the player's grief, in their burden, in the way they move through the world.

The first time they encounter the player, Rahn can tell: "You're carrying someone else's choice. Someone you loved is walking a path that leads to the same place it always leads. You think you can follow them. You can't. You can only let them walk it."

### About Detachment

Rahn has genuinely found peace. The world is beautiful when you stop expecting it to be fair. The journeys are rich. The solitude is real and deep. They're not unhappy.

But they're also absent. When communities need intervention, Rahn is elsewhere. When someone is hollowing and could still be stopped, Rahn doesn't reach out. Not from cruelty, but from the conviction that they can't stop it anyway. Why exhaust yourself fighting the current?

## Encounters with the Player (Wandering Path)

The player encounters Rahn at specific destinations on the wandering path. Not as a companion, but as a recurring presence. Each encounter offers a piece of Rahn's story and a mirror to the player's journey.

### Encounter 1: Early Wandering (Act 1)

**Location:** A natural spring in a forested valley (off the main paths)

**Context:** The player is exploring, newly alone, struggling with the weight of choice.

**Scene:** Rahn sits by the spring, bare feet in the water, drawing something on bark. They don't seem surprised when the player approaches. "You're carrying something heavy," Rahn observes. Not a question.

**What the Player Learns:** Rahn acknowledges what the player is experiencing without trying to fix it. Suggests that some burdens can't be put down. Only accepted.

**Rahn's Voice:** Direct, simple, without sentimentality. "The water keeps flowing regardless of what we're carrying. The question is whether you flow with it or whether you spend all your strength pushing back."

### Encounter 2: Mid-Wandering (Act 2)

**Location:** Ruins of a settlement recently abandoned

**Context:** The player has been gathering power, understanding, or following detachment. They've begun to see patterns.

**Scene:** Rahn is examining the ruins with quiet attention. They note the order of things: which structures were abandoned first, which were maintained longest, where families clustered. The environmental archaeology of a settlement that chose to hollow rather than leave.

**What the Player Learns:** Rahn explains, without judgment, what happened here. A community discovered magic. Used it eagerly. When the local mana depleted, they drew from themselves rather than wait for it to return. They transformed. "This happened a few months ago. But the shape of it is old. I've seen communities destroy themselves over fish, over timber, over water. This is the same thing. Magic just makes the destruction more complete."

**Rahn's Encounter with the Specific Settlement:** Rahn passed through this place *before* the hollowing, during their long wandering. It was a living settlement then. They came back recently and found it empty. They've spent time here reading the evidence—piecing together how quickly the community fell apart once magic entered the equation.

**Rahn's Voice:** "Fifteen years of walking taught me that people destroy themselves when they refuse to accept limitation. This past year taught me that magic makes it worse than I ever imagined."

### Encounter 3: Late Wandering (Act 2/3)

**Location:** A high overlook where multiple valleys are visible. A place of perspective.

**Context:** The player has deepened their understanding of the wandering path. They understand detachment. They're beginning to understand the cost.

**Scene:** Rahn sits on a stone, watching the landscape. The wind moves through valleys. Clouds move across ridges. Distant settlements are visible as small clusters of light at dusk.

The player asks (or Rahn volunteers): "Did it ever occur to you to return? To see what happened at your home?"

Rahn's response is measured. "Once, about five years ago. I went back to the coastal settlement." A pause. "It was smaller than I remembered. Fewer people. The ones who remained didn't recognize me. Ven's grave was overgrown. The dwelling we shared had been given to another family."

"I stood there and waited to feel something that would make me stay. I didn't feel it. So I left again."

A longer pause. Then:

"This past year, I've thought about going back again. Because now people there are using magic, same as everywhere. And I keep wondering—are they pushing too hard? Are they hollowing themselves the way that other settlement did? Am I going to walk back one day and find that the people I left behind have become something that isn't people anymore?"

"And then I think: would I do anything about it if they were? And the answer is probably no. And that's the thing I live with."

**What the Player Learns:** The cost of the wandering path is not just freedom but also powerlessness. Rahn chose to walk away from grief fifteen years ago. Now, with magic making that same pattern of self-destruction literal and irreversible, the stakes of their detachment are higher than ever. They know it. They accept it. That acceptance is both their peace and their failure.

**Rahn's Voice:** "The world will break people. You can't prevent it. You can only decide whether you'll break with them or whether you'll keep walking. I chose to keep walking. I've had a good journey. The sunsets are beautiful. Sometimes that's enough. And sometimes it isn't."

## Rahn's Flaw: The Cost of Detachment

Rahn's peace is real. Their wisdom is hard-won and true. But it was purchased by abandoning responsibility.

They don't regret it—regret would require believing they could have done something different. They don't. But the absence matters. Communities they passed through could have used their warnings. People they could have helped are now gone. Rahn is alive and at peace while others are broken or transformed.

**The Specific Wound:** The brother is walking the same path that Ven walked—different mechanism, same trajectory. Ven worked themselves to death for obligation. The brother is hollowing himself for the same reason: because stopping feels like surrender. Rahn sees this clearly. They could try to stop it, to warn the player, to do what they couldn't do for Ven. Instead, Rahn simply shares what they've learned and lets the player choose. Not from wisdom but from resignation.

If the brother transforms completely, Rahn will accept it the way they accepted Ven's death: as the cost of a choice they couldn't change. The hollowing is new. The heartbreak isn't.

## Voice and Dialogue Texture

Rahn speaks with the clarity of someone who has thought deeply about hard truths. They don't soften reality. But they don't weaponize it either.

**Example exchanges:**

> **Player:** "Can I stop my brother?"
>
> **Rahn:** "No. You can only decide whether you're going to keep trying and break with him, or whether you're going to let him go. Either way, you'll carry it."

> **Player:** "How did you live with it?"
>
> **Rahn:** "One day at a time. One sunrise at a time. Eventually enough days pass that it stops being a wound and becomes just a scar. Then even the scar fades into background. Not gone. Just... background."

> **Player:** "Is the wandering path the right one?"
>
> **Rahn:** "It's the path where you survive. Whether that's 'right' depends on what you believe you owe the world. I stopped believing I owed anything. That lets you be very free. It also lets you be very alone. You have to decide which one matters more."

## Structural Role

Rahn serves three critical functions:

1. **Proof that the human pattern repeats.** Rahn is the evidence that the brother isn't unique in his trajectory—not because people have hollowed before (the hollowing is terrifyingly new), but because the *drive* underneath it is ancient. People have always destroyed themselves through obligation and desperation. The brother is walking a road that Ven walked, that a hundred communities Rahn has witnessed walked, just with a more devastating mechanism.

2. **The wandering path's emotional anchor.** Rahn embodies the wandering path—freedom, detachment, beauty, solitude, and the specific loneliness of choosing yourself over connection.

3. **The perspective of someone who has already lost this fight.** Rahn lost Ven to the simple, slow version of self-destruction: overwork, obligation, refusal to stop. Now they're watching the brother pursue the magical version—faster, more total, more irreversible. Their inability to intervene isn't from inexperience with hollowing specifically. It's from a lifetime of learning that you can't stop someone who's chosen to give themselves away.
