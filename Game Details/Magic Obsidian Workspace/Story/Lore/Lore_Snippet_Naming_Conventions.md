# LORE SNIPPET — What They Call It: Naming Conventions and Magical Cultures

## The Principle

Every community that has discovered magic in the past year has given it a name. The name they chose reveals everything — how they found it, what they think it is, how they relate to it, and where their understanding breaks down. No two communities arrived at the same conclusion independently because no two communities had the same first experience. The word is a worldview compressed into a syllable.

When the player arrives at a new settlement and hears what they call magic, that single word is a diagnostic. It tells the player how these people think, what they've seen, what they haven't seen, and what they'll fight to defend as truth.

---

## The Names

### Magic

The most common term. The old-world word for the impossible, dusted off and applied to the newly possible. Communities that use this word tend to be the most pragmatic — they're not making grand claims about the nature of what they've found. They're labeling what they see. It works. They don't know why. They call it what their grandparents' stories would have called it.

This word carries the least ideological weight, which makes "magic" communities the easiest for outsiders to interact with. There's no theological framework to navigate, no cosmology to respect. Just people doing things they couldn't do a year ago and calling it the obvious thing. The downside is that pragmatism can mask shallow understanding. A community that shrugs and says "it's magic" may not be asking the questions that would keep them safe.

## Shelved for Future Exploration

### The Gift

A community with a passive, grateful orientation toward magic. It arrived. They didn't earn it. They can't explain it. They accept it. Casting is receiving, not taking.

This framework would make them instinctively cautious about overcasting because the concept of *taking* from a gift feels disrespectful. Potentially one of the safest communities in the world — not from understanding, but from temperament.

**Shelved because:** The emotional framework is compelling but needs more thought on what makes this community distinct in practice beyond general caution. May overlap with the Breath or Flow communities. Revisit when a specific narrative or encounter calls for it.

## Collision and Conflict

All of these communities exist simultaneously and are roughly the same age — about a year into their understanding. When they encounter each other, their frameworks collide with the heat of recent invention. Nobody's tradition is old enough to have earned the weight of authority. Everyone is equally certain and equally new.

A Divinity community meeting an Elemancy community sees heretics — people casting without prayer, without reverence, treating Her gift as a natural phenomenon. The Elemancers see superstitious fools attributing physical effects to an imaginary deity. A Flow practitioner watching a Script caster fumble with scrolls sees someone who has completely misunderstood the nature of what they're working with. The Script caster watching the Flow practitioner cast without visual reference sees someone who shouldn't be able to do what they're doing — and might conclude they're faking it.

These collisions are where some of the richest NPC interactions live. Not combat. Not quests. Just two groups of people who discovered the same thing at the same time and arrived at completely different conclusions, each convinced the other is dangerously wrong. The player navigates between them, accumulating fragments of every framework, unable to fully endorse or dismiss any of them — because at one year in, nobody has earned the right to certainty.
