# LORE SNIPPET — Runework: Kanji Inscribed on the Physical World

## The Theory

If a kanji held in the mind creates a funnel that magic flows through, then a kanji inscribed on a physical object should be able to do the same thing. The symbol is the interface. The shape is what magic recognizes. So carve it into steel, etch it into stone, stitch it into leather — the shape is still there. Magic should still read it.

It does. But not on its own.

## The Missing Element

A kanji inscribed on an object is inert. It's a shape — precise, correct, recognizable — but nothing flows through it. Magic sees the potential funnel but doesn't engage. The inscription sits there like an empty channel with no water.

The reason is the soul. When a person casts a spell, they provide two things simultaneously: the symbol held in the mind, and the living intent behind it. The soul is the spark — the thing that tells magic *this funnel is open, start flowing*. Without that spark, the symbol is just a drawing. A beautifully accurate drawing, maybe. But inert.

This is what protects the centrality of the human element in the magic system. The kanji is the shape. The soul is the ignition. Neither works without the other. A person without a symbol can't direct magic into a coherent effect. A symbol without a person can't activate at all. Every act of magic is a collaboration between a living soul and a meaningful form. That relationship is the engine. Everything else is built on top of it.

## Charging

Inscription becomes functional through charging. A person holds the corresponding kanji in their mind, makes contact with the inscribed object, and deliberately bridges the gap — their soul activates the inscription the way it would activate a cast spell, but instead of producing an immediate effect, the energy is channeled into the object and held there. The rune becomes a charged funnel, running on borrowed vitality.

The charge fades. How quickly depends on the complexity of the symbol, the precision of the inscription, the strength of the charge, and how actively the rune is being used. A simple enhancement on a tool might hold for days or weeks of regular use. A complex combat inscription on a weapon might deplete in hours of active fighting. The enchantment is not permanent. It's a loan.

This means runework is a relationship, not a transaction. Someone has to maintain the charge. A runed sword isn't a magic sword — it's a sword that someone is actively keeping magical through repeated investment of their own focus and energy. The object doesn't own the magic. It borrows it from a person who has to keep choosing to give it.

## The Cost

Charging a rune draws from the same sources as any other casting. If ambient mana is available, the charger channels environmental magic into the object — sustainable, safe, no different from casting a spell in a healthy area. The world gives, the person directs, the object holds.

If the environment is drained, the cost comes from the charger. Health, vitality, being — the same self-expenditure that drives hollowing in combat casting. A runecrafter working in a mana-thin environment is hollowing themselves the same way a battle mage does, just slower and less dramatically. The danger is arguably worse because it feels mundane. You're not fighting for your life. You're just maintaining tools. The cost accumulates invisibly because the act doesn't feel urgent.

This is the quiet version of the world's central lesson: taking from yourself is always available, always convenient, and always dangerous. A runecrafter who works carefully in mana-rich environments is fine. A runecrafter who pushes through depleted conditions because the village needs its tools recharged is making the same choice as the brother pushing through one more fight in a drained zone. The context is different. The mechanic is identical.

## Precision

Casting a spell requires holding a symbol clearly in the mind. The mental image doesn't need to be perfect — the soul provides interpretive flexibility, smoothing over minor imprecisions the way a fluent speaker glosses over a mispronunciation. The meaning comes through because the person *knows* the meaning.

Inscription has no such flexibility. The physical symbol is fixed. There's no living mind behind it moment-to-moment to correct for errors. The kanji on the object is exactly what magic reads, and if the shape is wrong — a stroke too short, a component misplaced, proportions slightly off — the funnel is distorted.

A slightly wrong inscription might not work at all. Magic sees a shape that almost matches a known funnel, finds no coherent meaning, and ignores it. That's the safe failure mode.

A slightly wrong inscription might also produce an unintended effect. The distorted shape resembles a different meaning — close to what was intended, but off in a way the crafter didn't anticipate. Magic reads the shape it was given, not the shape that was meant, and the result is something unexpected. Not necessarily catastrophic. But not what was asked for. And on a weapon or a piece of armor, "not what was asked for" can be dangerous.

This makes runecrafting a precision discipline. Not everyone who can cast can inscribe. The skills are related but distinct — one requires mental clarity and intent, the other requires physical exactness and deep familiarity with the structural details of each symbol. A good runecrafter is part calligrapher, part engineer, part scholar. They need to know not just what a kanji means, but exactly how every stroke contributes to that meaning, because in inscription, the strokes are all magic has to work with.

## Combinatorial Inscription

Two kanji inscribed together on the same object should produce the same combinatorial effect as two kanji cast together — a merged funnel that creates something new from the intersection of meanings. This follows directly from the casting system.

In practice, combinatorial inscription is significantly harder than combinatorial casting. When casting, the person holds both symbols simultaneously and their soul mediates the combination — intent and understanding smooth the intersection into a coherent merged effect. When inscribing, both symbols are fixed on the object, and the combination has to work purely through the physical relationship between the shapes. Placement matters. Proximity matters. The spatial relationship between the two inscriptions affects how magic reads their intersection.

This is where runecrafting becomes an art. A master runecrafter understands not just individual symbols but how symbols interact when placed in specific arrangements — overlapping, adjacent, concentric, sequential. The craft has depth that could take a lifetime to explore, and because the combinatorial space is enormous, no single crafter has mapped it fully. Regional traditions develop, guarded techniques emerge, and the knowledge path's collaborative body of work becomes even more valuable — a runecrafter with access to the mentor's research on pairings has a significant advantage over one working from trial and error alone.

## What This Means for the World

Runework is rare. Not because the theory is secret, but because the requirements are demanding — precise physical skill, deep symbol knowledge, access to healthy mana environments for safe charging, and the willingness to invest personal energy into objects on an ongoing basis. Most communities have at best one or two people capable of basic inscription. A skilled combinatorial runecrafter is a genuine rarity, someone entire settlements would compete to attract or retain.

Runed items are valuable not as permanent enchantments but as maintained tools. Owning a runed weapon means nothing if there's no one to recharge it. The object is only as useful as the relationship behind it — the crafter who made it, the charger who maintains it, the community that supports both. Runework is inherently communal. It binds people together through mutual dependence. A lone wanderer with a runed blade is carrying something that will eventually go dark unless they can charge it themselves, and self-charging in the field means drawing from whatever mana is available — or from themselves.

This ties runework to the community path naturally. A thriving village with a dedicated runecrafter, a healthy mana environment, and a culture of cooperation can maintain a modest inventory of functional runed tools — farming implements that work better, defensive wards on walls, preserved food storage. Nothing world-breaking. Just life made slightly better through sustained, careful magical investment. The kind of quiet, practical magic that doesn't make legends but makes a village survive.
