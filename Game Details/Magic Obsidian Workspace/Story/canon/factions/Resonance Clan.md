### Resonance

A community that discovered magic through collective experience. At some point in the past year, a large group of people came together — singing, mourning, celebrating, something that unified them emotionally — and the ambient magic responded. Not to any individual. To the group. To the combined emotional density of many souls acting as one.

This is genuinely touching something real and distinct from standard kanji casting. When a large group of people synchronize emotionally — truly feeling the same thing, moving together, becoming one in the moment — their souls do something that a single person's soul cannot. On some level of interaction with the ambient field, the souls connect. They form a collective surface that catches ambient magic the way a sail catches wind, where a single person is just a thread in the breeze. The result is magic that doesn't flow through a kanji funnel. It flows through the *group*, and the effects are more vague, more abstract, more unpredictable than anything a targeted spell produces.

This can't be faked or trivially reproduced. The participants have to genuinely feel it. Mechanical repetition of a song without emotional unity produces nothing. A group going through the motions is just a group making noise. The resonance requires authentic collective experience — shared grief, shared joy, shared purpose felt deeply enough that individual boundaries soften and the souls briefly act as one.

The Resonance community is probably the most vocal in their casting practice. They sing, they chant, they use sound as the medium through which collective focus is built and maintained. Individual members cast normally on their own — visualizing kanji, channeling through the funnel like anyone else. Someone casting a fire spell might shout "Fire!" while holding the kanji in their mind, the vocalization serving as personal focus rather than mechanical necessity. But their distinctive practice, the thing that defines them, is the collective casting that no other community has discovered.

What this community is working with, whether they know it or not, is closer to the Skelemancer's formation principle than to standard casting. The Skelemancer formed when dense ambient emotional energy organized magic without a conscious participant. The Resonance community is doing something similar but controlled — dense collective emotional energy organizing magic through many conscious participants acting as one. The mechanism is related. The intent and outcome are vastly different. But the underlying principle — that emotional density can shape magic outside the kanji funnel system — is the same.

This community might understand something about magic that nobody else does, including the researcher and mentor. Their practice points toward a dimension of the system that individual casting can't access. Whether that understanding ever becomes formalized or remains embedded in the lived experience of communal singing is an open question.

The effects of collective resonance are broad but shallow. When the singing truly connects — when the group achieves genuine emotional unity — the result washes over the area like warm water. Small wounds close. Bruises fade. A persistent cough clears. Sore muscles ease. The feeling is restoration, not intervention — closer to a long rest in a hot spring than to targeted medicine. Nobody is cured of anything serious. Nobody's broken bone is mended. But everyone within the effect feels distinctly better in the way that accumulates into something meaningful over time. A community that resonates regularly is healthier, more resilient, and recovers faster from hardship — not through any single dramatic act of healing, but through a constant, gentle maintenance that no individual caster with any known kanji has been able to replicate. The complexity of true healing — cellular repair, mending what is broken at a structural level — remains beyond what anyone has achieved through the funnel system. The Resonance community hasn't solved that problem either. They've just found something adjacent: a collective comfort that touches everything lightly rather than fixing anything deeply.### The Script

A community that found the kanji first — the physical symbols on old-world artifacts — and built their entire understanding around the written form. To them, magic is fundamentally textual. The symbols aren't an interface to something larger. They *are* the magic. The writing is the power. The ink on the page is what matters.

This community hasn't realized that you don't need to be visually engaging with the symbol to cast it. They believe the written form must be seen — looked at, read, visually held — for magic to work. The kanji on the page and the kanji in the mind must be present simultaneously. The eye and the soul working together.

In practice, this produces a distinctive casting style. Script casters carry scrolls — prepared kanji on portable surfaces, ready to be unrolled, stared at, and cast from. They whip out a scroll, lock their eyes on the symbol, and cast. The visual engagement reinforces the mental image, and the scroll becomes a rapid-access tool. A Script caster with a well-organized scroll kit can chain-cast spells quickly by flipping from one prepared symbol to the next, each visual contact refreshing and sharpening the mental image.

This is simultaneously a limitation and a genuine technique. The limitation: they're dependent on their scrolls. They believe they can't cast without visual reference, so they can't — the belief constrains them even though the constraint isn't real. Take away their scrolls and they're helpless, not because the magic stopped working but because they're certain it did.

The technique: using visual reference to reinforce mental clarity is actually effective. A caster who looks at a kanji while holding it in their mind's eye achieves a sharper, more precise visualization than one working from memory alone. The symbol is held simultaneously in the literal eye and the soul's eye, and the doubling strengthens the funnel. Script casters may achieve marginally better precision and consistency than equivalent casters working from pure memory, at the cost of needing their materials.

This community would have the strongest reverence for spell tomes and the deepest anxiety about preserving written knowledge. If the writing is destroyed, the magic is destroyed — that's their genuine belief. A library fire isn't a loss of reference material. It's a loss of *power itself*. They'd view the knowledge economy differently from anyone else: hoarding isn't about leverage, it's about preserving the source of all magic. Sharing isn't generosity, it's creating copies of something precious in case the original is lost.
