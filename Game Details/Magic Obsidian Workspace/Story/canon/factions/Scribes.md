### The Script

A community that found the kanji first — the physical symbols on old-world artifacts — and built their entire understanding around the written form. To them, magic is fundamentally textual. The symbols aren't an interface to something larger. They *are* the magic. The writing is the power. The ink on the page is what matters.

This community hasn't realized that you don't need to be visually engaging with the symbol to cast it. They believe the written form must be seen — looked at, read, visually held — for magic to work. The kanji on the page and the kanji in the mind must be present simultaneously. The eye and the soul working together.

In practice, this produces a distinctive casting style. Script casters carry scrolls — prepared kanji on portable surfaces, ready to be unrolled, stared at, and cast from. They whip out a scroll, lock their eyes on the symbol, and cast. The visual engagement reinforces the mental image, and the scroll becomes a rapid-access tool. A Script caster with a well-organized scroll kit can chain-cast spells quickly by flipping from one prepared symbol to the next, each visual contact refreshing and sharpening the mental image.

This is simultaneously a limitation and a genuine technique. The limitation: they're dependent on their scrolls. They believe they can't cast without visual reference, so they can't — the belief constrains them even though the constraint isn't real. Take away their scrolls and they're helpless, not because the magic stopped working but because they're certain it did.

The technique: using visual reference to reinforce mental clarity is actually effective. A caster who looks at a kanji while holding it in their mind's eye achieves a sharper, more precise visualization than one working from memory alone. The symbol is held simultaneously in the literal eye and the soul's eye, and the doubling strengthens the funnel. Script casters may achieve marginally better precision and consistency than equivalent casters working from pure memory, at the cost of needing their materials.

This community would have the strongest reverence for spell tomes and the deepest anxiety about preserving written knowledge. If the writing is destroyed, the magic is destroyed — that's their genuine belief. A library fire isn't a loss of reference material. It's a loss of *power itself*. They'd view the knowledge economy differently from anyone else: hoarding isn't about leverage, it's about preserving the source of all magic. Sharing isn't generosity, it's creating copies of something precious in case the original is lost.


This faction has no nearby neighbors. They are in contact with nobody.
