# LORE SNIPPET — The Fall

## What Happened

Approximately 50 years before the game begins, civilization collapsed. Not a slow decline — an abrupt severing. All modern technology rendered inert in an instant. Devices failed permanently. Infrastructure collapsed overnight. Production, communication, transportation, medicine — all ceased. Many people died. Others fled urban centers for wild regions.

Nobody knows why it happened. Nobody has a satisfying explanation. It simply ended.

## The Aftermath

Plant life reclaimed structures at an unnatural pace. Ruins were consumed within decades, swallowed by growth that seemed almost eager. Mass death at concentrated sites created conditions for ambient mana to pool and organize — a process that occurred passively over decades with no human involvement, seeding the land with dense magical energy long before anyone knew it was there.

Survivors formed small cooperative communities bound by proximity and necessity. Trust became local. Strangers were not welcomed easily. The mentality that emerged from the collapse was simple and absolute: community must be preserved at all costs. This attitude still shapes every settlement the player encounters — the suspicion of outsiders, the reluctance to share resources, the fierce loyalty to the people who kept you alive.

## The Gap

The Fall and the discovery of magic are separate events separated by roughly 50 years. Communities had time to stabilize after the collapse. People rebuilt routines. Raised children. Developed new norms. The world was dangerous but knowable — predators, weather, scarcity, the slow grind of survival without technology.

Then magic appeared, and destabilized everything again. The elders who vaguely remember the collapse now watch their grandchildren channel forces no one understands. The hard-won stability of two generations is fracturing under the weight of something nobody was prepared for.

## What People Remember

Almost nothing, firsthand. The collapse was 50 years ago. The people who lived through it as adults are dead. Those who remember it at all were children — they recall the fear, the flight, the sudden absence of everything familiar, but not the mechanisms or the cause. The Fall exists in living memory as trauma, not as history. It's a wound, not a lesson. Nobody learned anything from it because there was nothing to learn. The world just stopped working and never started again.
